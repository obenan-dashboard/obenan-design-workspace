This report asks whether the intended emotional promise travels across seven public locales. It evaluates semantic intent, linguistic risk, trust and responsibility implications, forms of address, text expansion, and native review needs. It does not decide product truth. Claims about assistant access, external execution, automation, or supported work remain subject to product and release proof.

## Executive verdict

The promise travels, but the English wording does not travel safely as a master string. Every locale can express continuing AI change and credible relief from personally tracking every change. Literal versions of “chase,” “on your behalf,” “handled for you,” and “peace of mind,” however, can add competition, desperation, legal representation, total responsibility, security, or guaranteed-outcome meanings.

The most portable promise is bounded companionship: Obenan keeps track of relevant change and carries specified ongoing work, while the customer remains informed and in control. This is a semantic brief, not a sentence to translate. “You do not have to chase it” may remain an English test candidate, but it should not become the source string for other locales. Portuguese Brazil and Portuguese Portugal require separate transcreation. Spanish requires an explicit target-market and form-of-address decision before writing. German, Dutch, and Turkish also need audience-specific address choices.

The February dataset is useful where it prioritizes clarity, customer transformation, guide behavior, consistency, and restraint. It is not reliable as a universal cross-cultural system. Its fixed voice ratios, national generalizations, unsupported neuroscience, fear mechanics, and unbounded automation claims should be removed. Current research does not justify national personality recipes. It does support intent-based native transcreation, calibrated trust, and testing whether people recover the intended responsibility boundary. Assistant access belongs in proof or detail only after the supported environments and functions are verified.

## Evidence boundaries

The report maintains four distinct layers:

- **A. Directly observed evidence:** What the local files, language guides, dictionaries, research papers, and current native-market pages actually contain.
- **B. February dataset claims:** What Diamond V1 asserts, including claims that are useful, weak, or contradicted by current direction.
- **C. Interpretation:** What the evidence suggests for Obenan. Interpretation is not a product fact or cultural law.
- **D. Recommendation:** A proposed operating rule or decision gate. Recommendations are not approved wording.

Current-market examples show that a construction is in use in a particular institutional context. They do not prove that Obenan customers prefer it, that it converts, or that it transfers unchanged into a commercial headline.

## What the February dataset gets right

The following Diamond V1 principles remain useful when stripped of universal formulas and unsupported certainty:

1. **Clarity before category machinery.** Lead with the customer's situation and desired change before mechanisms, dashboards, directories, or platform terms.
2. **Customer as protagonist, brand as capable guide.** This aligns with the current peer-like accompaniment direction when authority stays bounded.
3. **Transformation before feature inventory.** Relief from ongoing work is more portable than a list of tools or channels.
4. **Warmth plus competence.** Both matter in every locale, but no fixed numerical ratio is evidenced.
5. **Consistency and polish.** Terminology, proof, visual hierarchy, and responsibility language should agree across the journey.
6. **Restraint around jargon, competitor names, and manufactured scarcity.** This supports platform-neutral language and avoids pressure tactics.
7. **Adaptation is necessary.** The dataset is directionally right that language must feel native. Its own locale prescriptions, however, do not meet the evidence standard for doing that adaptation.

These are structural principles. They do not validate any particular headline, promise, emotional claim, or translation.

## What must be corrected, removed, qualified, or updated

### Remove

- Unsupported neuroscience and metabolism claims, including the claim that the brain burns about 800 calories through decision-making and broad statements that people are neurologically wired for one story formula.
- National or regional personality shortcuts, fixed cultural scores, and fixed authority-to-warmth ratios. A recent meta-analysis found only very small average benefits from cultural-value adaptation and concluded that the effect is not dependable enough to prescribe practitioner rules.
- Fear, shame, competitive panic, “stay ahead,” “fall behind,” and fabricated urgency. These amplify the concern instead of resolving it.
- Absolutes such as “every directory,” “every review,” “every language,” “never sleeps,” “whatever comes next,” “all handled automatically,” and “predictable outcomes.”
- Claims that affluent buyers do not compare, that discounting is universally wrong, or that one failure image must be paired with a fixed number of success images.
- Mechanical universals such as exactly two calls to action, exactly three repetitions, or a maximum of four process steps.

### Correct

- “No another dashboard” is not grammatical English. Even a corrected dashboard line would remain a secondary, proof-gated benefit and a category anchor, not a universal emotional promise.
- “On your behalf” must not stand alone. In several locales its closest ordinary wording signals representation or authority. State the supported object, scope, customer control, and any approval boundary.
- “Handled for you” must identify what is handled. Without an object it is readily heard as total completion or transferred responsibility.
- “Works through the assistant you prefer” must not imply universal compatibility, broad control, or execution. “Preferred” requires a current support matrix, and “works through” requires a function-by-function description.

### Qualify

- “Peace of mind” is an intended emotional payoff, not a guaranteed state. In translation it can move toward safety, security, certainty, or spiritual calm. Concrete proof must earn the feeling.
- “Keep up with AI” is understandable but frames AI as a single race. It should mean following relevant changes, not competing with AI or avoiding inferiority.
- “Your AI talks to our AI” is memorable but platform-anchored and technically expansive. It should not be used without verified architecture and scope.

### Update

- Replace literal-translation thinking with an intent brief, locale-specific form-of-address rules, native transcreation, semantic review, and comprehension testing.
- Treat Spanish as an unresolved locale target. A generic es route does not itself decide Spain Spanish, panregional Spanish, or a named market mix.
- Maintain Portuguese Brazil and Portuguese Portugal as distinct copy systems. They differ materially in forms of address and natural service voice.

## Locale assessment

### English

**Observed:** “Chase” naturally has a figurative sense of trying very hard to obtain something difficult. Clear-language guidance favors short sentences, audience language, and active voice, while localization guidance warns that idioms and colloquialisms are hard to carry across languages.

**Interpretation:** English can support a concise, direct second-person voice. The specific chase metaphor adds urgency and striving that the founder direction does not require. “On your behalf” sounds professional but can also imply delegated authority. “Handled for you” sounds warm only when the handled object is explicit. “Peace of mind” is familiar but generic. “Assistant” is readily understood, yet it still creates product-category expectations.

**Guidance:** Use English as one locale, not as the grammatical mold for the other six. If “chase” is tested, test whether readers infer competition, being behind, or total relief. Do not infer cross-locale viability from an English result.

### Spanish

**Observed:** Microsoft's Spain guide recommends a clear, concise, friendly voice and generally uses informal singular address. Its neutral-Spanish guide states that some products use informal address and others formal address, depending on product and audience. Spain plural usage also differs from common panregional practice. RAE and ASALE explicitly treat Spanish as pluricentric rather than uniform. Current Red.es language shows that “accompanying” businesses and personalized support are normal service concepts in Spain, but the source is public-program communication, not Obenan customer research.

**Interpretation:** A generic es route leaves a material decision unresolved. A Spain-first voice can sound marked elsewhere, while a flattened neutral voice can lose warmth or sound imported. Pace constructions equivalent to staying current can be natural, but “following the rhythm” intensifies the race frame and literal pursuit language is unsuitable. Wording equivalent to “in the name of” can imply representation. “Peace of mind” can drift toward calm or security without explaining work.

**Guidance:** Choose the target market or an explicitly panregional standard before drafting. Decide singular form of address and plural behavior in the locale brief. Preserve accompaniment without paternalism. Do not let Spain-specific pronouns silently become canon for the whole es route.

### German

**Observed:** Microsoft's current guide favors clear, friendly, concise rewriting over literal translation and uses formal second-person address as its general product default. Current IHK business language says the speed of digital developments makes it difficult for firms to keep pace, demonstrating that the pace construction is natural in context. The same page reports time and complexity as challenges, but this is a Berlin business survey, not proof about Obenan prospects.

**Interpretation:** A direct pace phrase is comprehensible but foregrounds capacity pressure. The common equivalent of “on your behalf” has explicit representation associations. A common “handled” verb often implies that a task is finished or settled, which can overstate an ongoing service. A common “peace of mind” substitute can move toward safety or certainty. German compounds and nominal phrases can lengthen headings and create hard wrap points.

**Guidance:** Make a deliberate formal-versus-informal address decision. Prefer concrete verbs and specified work over abstract service nouns. Keep sentences structurally simple, permit additional length, and test line breaks. Do not use a completion verb for continuous work unless the object and cadence are clear.

### Dutch

**Observed:** Microsoft's Dutch guide calls for clear, friendly, concise rewriting and notes both formal and informal forms of address. It describes a growing Netherlands tendency toward informal address while making audience and product decisive. KVK's current small-business guidance uses informal second person and concrete AI terminology. Official Taaladvies defines the ordinary “on behalf” term through acting in another's name, confirming a representation meaning.

**Interpretation:** Informal Dutch can feel capable and close, but it is not automatically right for every business audience. A concise verb for “keeping up” can also mean tracking or maintaining records, while alternatives can emphasize not falling behind. A common “handled” expression can imply that everything is arranged. Compounds create memorability and wrapping risk even when the total character count seems acceptable.

**Guidance:** Decide je or u through audience evidence, then use it consistently. Specify what change is tracked and what work is carried. Avoid a bare representation term and a universal “it is arranged” promise. Test compound-heavy headings in actual components.

### Turkish

**Observed:** Microsoft's Turkish guide recommends natural rewriting, simple everyday wording, and avoidance of unnecessarily formal or older vocabulary. It notes that pronouns are often omitted when the verb already identifies the subject and that short abbreviations can be unclear. Current KOSGEB communication uses the established term for artificial intelligence in formal institutional prose.

**Interpretation:** Literal pursuit language is especially forceful and can suggest running after something. A first-person “we handle it” construction can be warm, but its confidence can become total responsibility if scope is absent. Polite second-person morphology may carry the relationship without a repeated explicit pronoun. AI terminology is not the issue; using it as a category-heavy lead is.

**Guidance:** Use contemporary vocabulary, natural word order, and a deliberate politeness level. Let morphology do work rather than forcing English pronoun repetition. Transcreate the release from monitoring, not the chase image. Attach all taking-care-of language to exact supported work and customer control.

### Portuguese Brazil

**Observed:** Microsoft's Brazilian Portuguese guide supports intent-based rewriting, commonly addresses the reader directly, discourages impersonal “user” language, and allows first-person plural to create a more personal, reassuring voice. Current Brazilian official usage shows that a construction equivalent to following technological evolution is ordinary in institutional language.

**Interpretation:** Direct warmth is available without copying English syntax. Literal “running after” language suggests catching up from behind or strenuous pursuit. The ordinary “in the name of” construction signals representation. A generic taking-care-of verb can suggest total service ownership. “Peace of mind” can move toward safety or reassurance and needs concrete grounding.

**Guidance:** Use a Brazil-native form-of-address and rhythm. Prefer active, specific service verbs and avoid redundant pronouns. Do not reuse Portuguese Portugal copy with spelling substitutions. Preserve customer agency explicitly where Obenan performs supported work.

### Portuguese Portugal

**Observed:** Microsoft's Portuguese Portugal guide favors natural intent over source-language structure and generally avoids explicit personal “you,” especially explicit você, by using implicit or third-person constructions. Ciberdúvidas documents that explicit você can be socially or regionally marked and may be heard as impolite by some speakers. Current IAPMEI business communication uses concise relief framing around AI and a construction equivalent to accompanying technological evolution. These examples demonstrate available language patterns, not recommended Obenan copy.

**Interpretation:** Reusing a Brazilian second-person voice can sound non-native or socially wrong. A direct equivalent of “on your behalf” remains representational. “Handled for you” can become formal, impersonal, or total depending on the construction. “Peace of mind” can sound like a security assurance. Copy may need more grammatical room to sound natural without explicit pronouns.

**Guidance:** Commission a Portugal-native transcreation independently from Brazil. Prefer implicit address where natural. Test whether reassurance remains personal without becoming formal or distant. Treat cross-Atlantic similarity as a false economy, not a reason to share a final string.

## Universal semantic intent and native adaptation

### Non-negotiable semantic intent

Every locale should preserve all of the following meanings:

1. AI-related conditions continue to change.
2. The customer does not need to personally monitor every relevant change.
3. Obenan carries specified, supported ongoing work rather than “everything.”
4. The customer remains informed and retains control, including approval where the product requires it.
5. Obenan does not guarantee discovery, recommendation, ranking, indexing, revenue, bookings, or execution in external systems.
6. The relationship feels like capable accompaniment, not instruction from a superior or rescue of an incapable customer.
7. No named platform is necessary to understand the leading promise.
8. Assistant-based access, if mentioned, is a verified access mode rather than the definition of Obenan.

### Elements that require native adaptation

- Form of address, pronoun use, and politeness level
- Whether reassurance is best expressed through a direct promise, a process verb, or evidence
- The verb for monitoring change and the verb for carrying recurring work
- Grammatical agency and who appears as the actor in the sentence
- The distinction between working for someone's benefit and legally representing them
- The local term for AI and, only where needed, AI assistant
- Headline rhythm, clause order, alliteration, and memorability
- Character count, word count, compounds, and mobile line breaks
- The amount of explicit explanation needed to keep responsibility bounded

Equal intent does not require equal syntax, equal word count, or an identical metaphor.

## Length and memorability constraints

English is often unusually compact. W3C guidance warns that short English interface strings can expand by 200 to 300 percent in translation in some cases, and cites substantial expansion examples in German and Portuguese. The examples are older interface data, not forecasts for these exact sentences, but the direction is relevant. German and Dutch compounds can also produce long unbreakable units.

Practical rules:

- Do not set one fixed English character cap as the quality threshold for all locales.
- Allocate flexible width and allow natural reflow in hero, navigation, buttons, cards, and proof labels.
- Evaluate memorability as one recoverable idea, not as equal word count.
- Avoid stacked metaphors. AI change, chasing, hands, guides, assistants, and dashboards should not all compete in one short unit.
- Test desktop and narrow mobile layouts with the real locale strings, not machine-expanded pseudo-copy alone.
- Never shorten a locale by deleting the responsibility boundary or product scope.
- Recheck accessibility, reading order, and line-break meaning after localization.

## Trust, agency, and responsibility

Trust should be calibrated to demonstrated capability, not maximized through confidence language. The trust-in-automation literature, NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and OECD AI Principles converge on context, transparency, limitations, accountability, and human oversight. They do not prescribe homepage copy, but they establish why unbounded autonomy language is risky.

Agency language is also not semantically neutral. Experiments have found that agentive versus non-agentive phrasing can alter attention, memory, blame, or responsibility judgments in specific settings. A preregistered replication confirmed the original blame effect in English but found mixed, smaller, or absent results in Croatian. The relevant conclusion is modest: grammar can change who appears responsible, and effects do not transfer uniformly across languages. This supports native semantic review. It does not justify predictions about any of Obenan's seven audiences.

For Obenan, a safe responsibility pattern names three roles:

- **Obenan:** monitors relevant change and performs defined supported work.
- **Customer:** remains informed, controls the relationship, and approves where required.
- **External system:** remains outside Obenan's authority unless actual delivery and receipt are verified.

## Practical rules for Obenan

1. Write one semantic brief and seven native solutions. Do not translate an English slogan word for word.
2. Name the recurring work before using any taking-care-of or on-behalf language.
3. Keep the customer's competence intact. Remove language that presumes overload, ignorance, lateness, a staffing plan, or current tool use.
4. Use companionship as relationship behavior, not as a sentimental metaphor that hides scope.
5. Keep named assistants and platforms out of leading copy. Put verified compatibility in proof or product detail.
6. Pair relief with control: what Obenan does, what the customer sees, and when approval is required.
7. Treat abstract emotional outcomes as earned effects. Evidence should precede “peace of mind.”
8. Maintain a locale glossary that marks permitted product terms, forbidden overclaims, form of address, and responsibility verbs.
9. Allow locale-specific length and grammar while preserving the same claim boundary.
10. Re-review every locale when the English intent, product scope, or assistant-support matrix changes.

## Illustrative safe and unsafe language patterns

The following English examples test semantic boundaries only. They are not approved copy and must not be translated as source strings.

### Safer patterns

- “AI keeps changing. Obenan keeps the supported work moving, with you in control.”
- “You do not need to track every AI change yourself. Obenan handles the supported ongoing work and shows you what changed.”
- “Obenan keeps watch on relevant changes and prepares the next supported step for your review.”

Why these are safer: they name change, limit the work, preserve control, and avoid a guaranteed business outcome.

### Unsafe patterns

- “Stay ahead of AI before competitors leave you behind.”
- “We handle everything automatically, in every assistant.”
- “Your preferred assistant can run Obenan for you.”
- “Peace of mind, guaranteed.”
- “Obenan acts in your name wherever your business appears.”

Why these are unsafe: they use fear or shame, universal scope, unverified compatibility, guaranteed emotion, or broad representation.

| Historic localization evidence may show a route exists. | Route existence does not prove current copy quality, product parity, or native approval. | Review actual current strings, supported behavior, metadata, and mobile rendering per route. |

## Native-review protocol

1. **Freeze the evidence brief.** Product owner supplies the supported-work inventory, preparation-versus-execution boundary, approval model, assistant support matrix, and prohibited outcome claims. Copy work stops if these are unresolved.
2. **Issue a semantic intent sheet.** Include the eight non-negotiable meanings above, the target audience and market, forbidden meanings, form-of-address decision, and examples labeled unsafe. English wording is reference context, not a sentence to preserve.
3. **Commission native transcreation.** Use a market-native senior copywriter for each locale. Spanish must match the chosen target. Portuguese Brazil and Portuguese Portugal must have separate owners. Machine translation may assist exploration but cannot be the deliverable.
4. **Create an independent semantic gloss.** A second qualified reviewer explains each candidate's literal sense, implied actor, scope, register, responsibility, and emotional reading in English. This is a meaning audit, not a demand for literal backtranslation.
5. **Run the risk screen.** Reject any candidate that introduces shame, race or behindness, total autonomy, legal representation, guaranteed safety or outcomes, unverified assistant compatibility, platform anchoring, or an assumption about the customer's skills, staffing, or current behavior.
6. **Test comprehension with the intended native audience.** Ask what Obenan does, what it does not do, who stays responsible, what requires approval, and what any assistant wording promises. Approval requires recovery of every non-negotiable meaning and no material false capability inference. Do not substitute a universal sample-size rule for research design appropriate to the decision.
7. **Perform product, legal, and claim review.** Product verifies every function and support statement. Legal or compliance review assesses representation, security, guarantee, and responsibility implications where material.
8. **Verify the rendered experience.** Test actual strings on desktop and narrow mobile for wrapping, truncation, hierarchy, accessibility, reading order, and metadata. Pseudo-localization is supplementary, not a replacement for native strings.
9. **Approve and version each locale separately.** Record writer, reviewer, market, product evidence version, date, unresolved concerns, and approver. An English approval does not approve another locale.
10. **Trigger re-review on change.** Any change to semantic intent, supported work, assistant coverage, external execution, or English source context reopens affected locale approvals.

## Evidence limits

- No first-party corpus of Obenan customer language was available by locale.
- No native interviews or comprehension tests were performed in this research task.
- No evidence supports a single cultural score, national personality, fixed warmth ratio, or deterministic persuasion rule for any locale.
- Microsoft style guides describe one vendor's localization conventions. They are useful operational evidence, not language law or proof of Obenan preference.
- Official current-market examples establish usage in institutional genres, not commercial effectiveness or customer preference.
- The experimental agency literature concerns eyewitness memory and blame, not SaaS positioning. It supports caution, not behavioral prediction.
- W3C expansion figures are planning signals, not exact forecasts for the proposed semantic intent.
- The generic Spanish route target remains unspecified.
- Current proof for preferred-assistant coverage, no-dashboard access, and external-system execution was not present in the governed research workspace.

## Final recommendations

1. Treat the English direction as a semantic brief, not a master translation.
2. Keep “chase” as an English-only test candidate and prohibit literal pursuit metaphors elsewhere.
3. Do not use “on your behalf” or “handled for you” without a verified object, scope, control, and approval boundary.
4. Decide the Spanish market and form of address before copy is commissioned.
5. Maintain fully separate Portuguese Brazil and Portuguese Portugal transcreation and approval paths.
6. Approve a locale-specific address policy for German, Dutch, Turkish, Spanish, and both Portuguese variants.
7. Keep assistant access and no-dashboard benefits in proof or product detail until the current support matrix is verified.
8. Treat peace of mind as an earned secondary effect, supported by concrete evidence rather than guaranteed.
9. Design for natural expansion, compounds, reflow, and locale-specific memorability.
10. Require two-person native semantic review, audience comprehension testing, product claim review, rendered QA, and separate versioned approval for every locale.
